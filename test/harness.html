The quiet collapse of the shared bookshelf

For twenty years the public library catalogue was the closest thing the web had to a common index of printed knowledge, and almost nobody noticed when it stopped being maintained. The story of that decay is not one of malice but of budgets, and it explains a great deal about why finding an out-of-print title is harder today than it was in 2004.

Librarians in three countries described the same pattern to us: a cataloguing system bought in the late nineties, a vendor acquired twice, and a migration that was quietly postponed every budget cycle until the institutional memory of how it worked had retired. The result is a catalogue that still answers queries but no longer learns anything new about the collection it describes.1

What replaced it was not a better catalogue but a dozen partial ones, each owned by a different vendor, none of them obliged to talk to the others. A reader looking for a 1978 monograph now searches four systems and trusts whichever answers first.2

The audit dates matter more than the record counts. A catalogue that has not been audited in fifteen years is not a catalogue; it is a snapshot with a search box bolted to the front of it, and the gap between what it claims to hold and what is actually on the shelf grows every year that nobody checks.
